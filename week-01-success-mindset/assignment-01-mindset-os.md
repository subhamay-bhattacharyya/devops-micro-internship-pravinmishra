# Assignment 1. What is something you believe to be true that most people around you would disagree with?

### Rules

* No "safe" answers.
* Must be your real belief (not copied from internet).
* Minimum 50 words.

**Hint:** What do you believe about career, money, learning, discipline, relationships, health, success, life, tech industry, etc. that most people don't agree with?

## Answer

**Most people think cloud certifications are just resume padding. I believe they're the fastest way to systematically close knowledge gaps and build muscle memory in production-grade architecture.**

Everyone around me talks about "learning by doing," but I've seen too many engineers in real environments making expensive decisions because they never sat down with the fundamentals. Certifications force you to understand why things work, not just that they work. AWS SCS-C03 taught me more about organizational security patterns in three months than years of ad-hoc implementations. The exam preparation itself—drilling policy logic, understanding edge cases, studying failure modes—that's where the real learning happens.

But certifications are only half the equation. I pair them with hands-on projects—whether it's building multi-account landing zones with Terraform, designing SCP governance patterns, or shipping open-source infrastructure modules. That's where knowledge crystallizes. The exam teaches the what, projects teach the how, and the friction between them teaches the why. It's not sexy, but it's efficient.

# Assignment 4. Have you ever cut corners (unethical / dishonest / shortcut behavior — not necessarily illegal)? If yes, how did it make you feel?

### Important

You don't need to write the full story.

Focus on the feeling:

* guilt
* fear
* shame
* stress
* regret
* numbness
* etc.

This is about self-awareness, not judgment.

### Answer Format

**Yes / No**

If Yes:

**What emotion did you feel?** (minimum 50–100 words)

## Answer

**Yes**

**What emotion did I feel?**

Anxiety that never fully went away. 

Early in my career at Genpact, I shipped an Oracle e-Business Suite Interface without fully validating the transformation logic across all edge cases. I documented it as "tested" when I knew I'd only tested the happy path. For weeks, I carried a low-level dread—not guilt exactly, but a constant background hum of "when will this break in production?" It didn't manifest as panic; it was worse. It was a numb, persistent fear that I'd eventually be exposed as someone who didn't care about the work.

The worst part wasn't getting caught (I wasn't). It was that I *remembered* doing it. Every time I reviewed someone else's code rigorously, every time I obsessed over validation logic in later projects, I was compensating for that moment. I realized I'd rather spend 10 extra hours getting it right than spend months living with that anxiety.

That's why I pair certifications with projects now. Why I document obsessively. Why I open-source code that I've tested thoroughly. It's not virtue signaling—it's the opposite of cutting corners. It's the only way I've found to work without that background dread.

The feeling taught me something objective: shortcuts don't save time. They just move the cost forward. You either pay the cost upfront through rigor, or you pay it later through anxiety and rework.

### Distraction Rules

Examples:

#### My Distraction Rules


### Phone Rules (5–7am Deep Work)
* Phone on silent, screen-down on desk (visible but muted)
* No Slack, email, or text notifications during session
* Social media apps **deleted from phone** (or use app blockers)
  * LinkedIn app: Muted
  * Twitter app: Deleted
  * Access only via browser (friction = deterrent)
* Capgemini urgent contacts: Whitelist only (team, manager)
* Check messages at 7:15am (post-session buffer)

### Social Media Rules
* **During 5–7am:** Zero access (apps deleted)
* **After 7am:** 5-minute LinkedIn/Twitter check only (purposeful, not browsing)
* **No social media during DMI sessions** (lunchtime/evening)
* **Saturday morning (8–10am):** No social media, code-only focus
* Schedule LinkedIn posts Sunday evening (don't draft live)

### Environment Setup (5–7am Sessions)
* **Location:** Private space (home office, quiet room)
* **Headphones:** On (music or white noise, no podcasts/videos)
* **Desk:** Only laptop + terminal + one reference document open
* **Notifications:** All off (Slack, Teams, Outlook, system)
* **Browser:** No Twitter/LinkedIn tabs open (use app blockers if needed: Freedom, Cold Turkey)

### Context Switching Rules
* **One task per session:** Don't jump between cert study → coding
* **No email checking** during 5–7am block
* **No "quick" Phone Check** mid-session
* **Timer:** 90-min focus → 5-min break pattern (if doing 2-hour blocks, split at 90min)

### Session Integrity
* **No meetings scheduled 5–8am** (block calendar)
* **No Slack messages answered** until 7:15am
* **Family/household:** "Do not disturb" signal (closed door, headphones visible)
* **Capgemini work:** Starts at 9am or later (gives 2-hour buffer post deep work)

### Weekly Audit
* **Sunday 6pm:** Check if distraction rules held (aim: 90%+ compliance)
* **Metric:** Sessions completed vs. sessions scheduled
* **If <80%:** Adjust rules (stricter or realistic)

### Punishment/Reward
* **If session completed:** 5-min guilt-free social media after 7:15am
* **If distracted:** No social media until next session
* **Weekly win:** 5/6 sessions completed = publish a LinkedIn post about the work (intentional, not reactive)
